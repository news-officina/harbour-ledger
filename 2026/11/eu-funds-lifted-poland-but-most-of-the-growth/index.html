The Harbour Ledger

News from the quay up.

EU funds lifted Poland, but most of the growth came from elsewhere

Two decades of membership brought 181 billion euro net and growth far above the EU average. The funds' own figures credit only a small share of it, and the bill for the next budget round is being set now.

1 November 2026

A study on ewaluacja.gov.pl, the Polish government's evaluation portal, breaks it. In Poland's richest regions, only 13 to 15 per cent of the narrowed distance to the EU average came from Cohesion Policy investments.

Most of the catch-up happened without the funds. The rest of what closed the gap came from something else, whatever it was. The study measures the funds against a world without them.

The ewaluacja.gov.pl page estimates the impact by running two scenarios, one with EU money and one without, on Statistics Poland data, ministry studies and Eurostat data for 2004 to 2021. For the cross-country comparison, data from Romania, Malta and Bulgaria were unavailable.

The question is what two decades of EU membership money changed in Poland, and who pays the next instalment.

The question matters now because the money keeps flowing and the bill comes due at home. Statistics Poland's president said the membership years brought many economic and social changes, enhanced by open borders, the single market and EU funds.

Janusz Lewandowski, a MEP and former EU budget commissioner, said the fundamental benefit is access to the free European market. "To fundamentalna korzyść," he said.

Two per cent of GDP

The ledger first. The payments averaged 2.0 per cent of nominal GDP each year from 2004 to 2021.

Poland's real GDP grew 99.1 per cent between 2004 and 2021, the same study shows. The EU-27 average was 25.2 per cent.

A study of Polish data from 1998 to 2007 found that emigration raised the wages of those who stayed. High- and medium-skilled workers saw the gains, the groups with the largest relative outmigration rates, that study found.

The flow has since turned round. The OECD reports that after two decades of outflows, Poland has become a destination country, with most foreign-born workers coming from Ukraine and Belarus. "Immigration has increased but remains moderate," it said.

The ewaluacja.gov.pl page notes that Poland's average annual growth of 3.9 per cent "stemmed to a significant extent" from EU funds, so crediting growth solely to accession overstates it.

The Council's negotiating box

The Council's negotiating box totals 746,734 million euro, with 705,250 million euro inside National and Regional Partnership Plans.

Freedom House found that illiberal leaders can use EU funds to sustain patronage networks without risking financial collapse.

Member states with a direct land border with Russia or Belarus get a factor of 1.25 under the border management allocation.

Polish authorities told the IMF that the planned ETS2 carbon pricing scheme unfairly shifts decarbonisation costs onto lower income countries. They said it should not proceed in its current design.

The room for paying is narrow. A fund staff report projects Poland's deficit at 7 per cent of GDP. Fund staff expect no rule-driven adjustment while the defence escape clause holds.

What the funds changed was measured. The cost of the next instalment is being set now.

Money in, money out

Business Insider, citing the Finance Ministry, calculated that over 16 years Poland received more than 181 billion euro and paid in 58 billion euro.

Patryk Jaki, an MEP, argued the balance reads differently. His report, presented in the Sejm that month, concluded that Poland lost 535 billion złoty over the same 16 years.

The claim rests on a wider argument: gross inflows mean little if what Poland buys to earn them costs more. Jaki's figures counted the imported inputs behind EU exports.

The finding did not stand unchallenged. The fact-checking portal Wojownicy Klawiatury said the report combines incomparable numbers. "Zestawiane są nieporównywalne liczby," it wrote.

Jaki's framing is the strongest case against the ledger, stated to the parliament that votes on the next envelope.

Big money, partial answer

The record shows big money and a partial answer. Cohesion spending ran at two per cent of GDP a year alongside growth far ahead of the EU average. But the funds' own study credits only a small share of the catch-up in the richest regions to the investments.

The same study shows the funds' effect weakened and turned technically negative in 2015 to 2018. The old budget round had ended, and projects under the new one were still small. The flow of money and the pace of growth do not move together.

The counter-ledger of losses rests on numbers fact-checkers called incomparable. So the gains have a measured answer; the losses do not.

Who pays next is the open part. The government disputes the carbon price it says falls on lower-income countries. Statistics Poland calls its own account an illustration, with rounding and simplifications.

The Council's own negotiations state that 'nothing is agreed until everything is agreed', and the total binds no delegation.

IMF staff note that the defence escape clause has been activated, and see no rule-driven adjustment before 2029.

So the border factor, the ETS2 dispute and the rule-of-law conditions all sit inside a box where every number can still be traded away.
